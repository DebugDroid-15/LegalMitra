## 1. Statutory Foundations

The Trade Marks Act, 1999 (Act No. 47 of 1999) received Presidential assent on 30 December 1999 and was brought into force on **15 September 2003** (S.O. 1048(E)), repealing the Trade and Merchandise Marks Act, 1958.

### Key Statutory Innovations of the 1999 Act
- Registration of trade marks for **services** (Classes 35 to 45).
- Protection of **well-known trade marks** across unrelated goods/services (Section 11(2)).
- Extension of trademark term from 7 years to 10 years (Section 25).
- Codification of action for infringement of unregistered trade marks (passing off preserved under Section 27(2)).
- Enhanced special jurisdiction: Plaintiff can institute infringement suits in district court where plaintiff resides or carries on business (Section 134(2)), deviating from CPC Section 20.

## 2. Madrid Protocol Accession (Act 40 of 2010)

Enacted on 21.09.2010; commenced on **08.07.2013** via S.O. 1999(E).  
Inserted **Chapter IVA (Sections 36A to 36G)**:
- Enables Indian trademark owners to secure international protection across 130+ member countries via a single international application filed with the Indian Trade Marks Registry.
- Enables foreign applicants designating India under the Madrid Protocol to obtain protection subject to Section 9 and 11 examination by the Indian Registrar.

## 3. Landmark Precedent: Cadila Health Care Ltd. v. Cadila Pharmaceuticals Ltd. (2001) 5 SCC 73

The Supreme Court established the heightened test for deceptive similarity in medicinal products:
1. **Stricter Scrutiny for Medicines**: Ordinary consumer confusion tests are insufficient for drugs. Even if two medicinal preparations fall under different therapeutic categories or require doctor prescriptions (Schedule H drugs), phonetic or visual similarity ('Falcigo' vs 'Falcitab') cannot be tolerated.
2. **Public Health Hazard**: Confusion in medicines can result in severe illness or death.
3. **Diverse Literacy Context**: In India, where medicines are often dispensed over handwritten prescriptions and purchased by consumers with diverse literacy levels, confusingly similar brand names must be prohibited.
